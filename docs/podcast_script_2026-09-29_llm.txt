Combined chlorogenic acid and baicalin supplementation reduces PRRSV‑induced disease in weaned piglets by reshaping immune responses and gut microbiota, according to a study in Microbiology Spectrum (PubMed). Researchers fed piglets a diet containing the two plant compounds and observed lower clinical scores, reduced viral load, and attenuated pro‑inflammatory cytokine production compared with controls. Metagenomic analysis revealed increased beneficial bacteria and decreased pathogenic taxa, correlating with improved intestinal barrier integrity. The findings suggest that this dietary strategy could mitigate PRRSV impact and offer a non‑antibiotic approach for swine health management.

The study asks when compressing the high‑dimensional intestinal microbiome into a lower‑dimensional representation still yields the same intervention decisions as using the full data. It shows that if functional redundancy, ecological filtering and niche construction cause taxonomically distinct communities to map onto similar host phenotypes, then a compressed microbiome model can preserve the optimal choice of host‑directed or microbial interventions. Conversely, when compression obscures functionally unique taxa that drive divergent host responses, intervention predictions diverge. By linking observation‑control mismatch to these ecological principles, the work provides a criterion for when microbiome dimensionality reduction is safe for guiding therapeutic strategies. Source: PubMed — Bio Systems.

Researchers introduce new statistical and bioinformatic methods to tackle compositional data problems that impede interpretation of microbiome changes in clinical safety assessments. Published in PubMed — Journal of Food Protection, the work highlights how amplicon and shotgun sequencing generate relative abundance profiles that violate standard assumptions, leading to biased inferences. By applying log‑ratio transformations, robust variance‑stabilizing techniques, and composition‑aware regression models, the authors show improved detection of true microbial perturbations linked to health outcomes. The approach aims to make safety evaluations of microbiome‑targeted interventions more reliable and reproducible.

Gut metabolites can either boost or blunt CAR‑T cell activity, according to a study of 129 lymphoma patients from three German centers published in Cell. Using shotgun metagenomics and targeted metabolomics, researchers identified specific microbial‑derived molecules that enhance CAR‑T proliferation and tumor killing, while others suppress cytokine production and promote exhaustion. The opposing effects help explain why antibiotic exposure or dysbiosis correlates with poorer outcomes in nearly half of recipients. The findings suggest that modulating the microbiome could improve CAR‑T durability and response rates.
