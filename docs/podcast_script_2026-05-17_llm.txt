Antenatal depressive symptoms can harm a child’s neurodevelopment by disrupting the mother’s gut microbiota during pregnancy, according to a study in Gut Microbes. Researchers found that pregnant women reporting higher depression scores showed distinct shifts in intestinal bacterial communities, and these microbial changes correlated with poorer cognitive and behavioral outcomes in their infants. The work highlights a potential pathway whereby maternal mental health influences fetal brain development through the gut‑brain axis, suggesting that targeting microbiome health in pregnancy might mitigate neurodevelopmental risks linked to prenatal depression.

Antenatal depressive symptoms harm offspring neurodevelopment by disrupting the mother’s gut microbiota during pregnancy, according to a multicenter prospective cohort of 2,053 pregnant women published in Gut Microbes via PubMed. Researchers found that elevated depressive symptoms in any trimester were significantly associated with alterations in maternal gut microbial composition, which in turn correlated with poorer neurodevelopmental outcomes in the children. The study highlights a microbiota‑mediated pathway linking maternal mental health to infant brain development, suggesting that targeting gut microbiome health could mitigate the developmental risks posed by antenatal depression.

Researchers show that the acetylation degree of konjac glucomannan directly shapes its impact on gut microbiota and prediabetic outcomes. By preparing KGM samples with low, medium and high acetyl content and feeding them to mice on a high‑fat diet, they found that moderately acetylated KGM most effectively increased beneficial Bifidobacteria and Lactobacillus, reduced hepatic gluconeogenesis, and lowered fasting glucose and insulin resistance. Low‑acetylated KGM had weaker prebiotic effects, while highly acetylated forms showed diminished viscosity and reduced microbial fermentation. The study, published in Carbohydrate Polymers via PubMed, provides the first mechanistic link between KGM acetylation pattern, microbial modulation, and glucose homeostasis, highlighting a tunable dietary fiber for diabetes prevention.

Bacteroides ovatus AP1, isolated from the gut microbiota of four midlife women, can break down and use both low‑ and high‑molecular‑weight chondroitin sulfate in an in‑vitro fermentation system, showing that this common glycosaminoglycan is a fermentable substrate for a prominent intestinal Bacteroides strain. The study, published in Carbohydrate Polymers and indexed in PubMed, used batch cultures with LMWCS and HMWCS to measure gas production, short‑chain fatty acid profiles, and bacterial growth, revealing comparable degradation rates despite the size difference. These results suggest that dietary chondroitin sulfate may reach the colon intact and be metabolized by specific gut microbes, potentially influencing host health in midlife women.

Specnuezhenide, a glycoside isolated from Ligustrum lucidum, reduces metabolic‑associated fatty liver disease in mice by reshaping gut microbiota and activating the PPARα signaling pathway. In the study, treatment with the compound lowered liver steatosis, inflammation and fibrosis markers, while increasing beneficial bacterial taxa and PPARα‑target gene expression. These findings link the natural product’s hepatoprotective action to a microbiota‑dependent metabolic reprogramming that promotes fatty acid oxidation. The work appears in PubMed‑indexed Diabetic Medicine, a journal of the British Diabetic Association, highlighting a potential microbiome‑targeted strategy for MAFLD/MASH.

A high‑salt diet drives calcium oxalate kidney stone formation not just by raising urinary calcium but by reshaping the gut microbiota to boost inflammatory pathways that generate trimethylamine and its oxidized form, TMAO. Researchers reporting in the FASEB Journal via PubMed showed that mice fed excess NaCl had altered gut bacteria, increased TMA/TMAO production, heightened intestinal inflammation, and more CaOx crystal deposition in kidneys. Antibiotic depletion of the microbiota blocked the stone‑promoting effect, confirming a gut‑dependent mechanism. The work links dietary salt, microbial metabolism, and inflammation to stone risk beyond simple calcium excretion.

Plant‑microbiome interactions shed new light on how plants regulate iron‑sulfur (Fe‑S) cluster metabolism, according to a study in the Journal of Experimental Botany accessed via PubMed. Researchers found that root‑associated microbes can modulate the plant’s iron and sulfur uptake pathways, thereby influencing the assembly and stability of Fe‑S clusters essential for photosynthesis, respiration and redox balance. While the core Fe and S assimilation genes are well known, this work reveals a microbial‑mediated crosstalk that fine‑tunes cluster biogenesis under fluctuating nutrient conditions, offering a fresh perspective on plant metabolic resilience.

Viral communities in Macao’s urbanized coastal habitats serve both as mirrors of the local antibiotic resistome and as vectors that spread resistance genes, according to a shotgun metagenomic study of 49 samples published in the Journal of Hazardous Materials (PubMed). Researchers found that bacteriophages isolated from water, sediment and sewage carried a diverse array of antibiotic resistance genes, with patterns matching those of bacterial hosts across the sampled sites. This dual role highlights viruses as overlooked contributors to ARG dissemination in densely populated, mixed‑use environments, urging their inclusion in surveillance and mitigation strategies.

Researchers have isolated a human‑derived bacterial strain from the gut that appears to mediate anti‑tumor immunity, offering a new microbial lever to boost cancer immunotherapy. The study, published in iScience and indexed via PubMed, notes that while the gut microbiota is a promising target for improving immunotherapy outcomes, the specific microbial molecules driving this effect have remained poorly defined. By identifying this human‑derived strain, the work provides a concrete candidate for further mechanistic exploration, though the exact immunomodulatory compounds it produces are still under investigation. Source: PubMed — iScience.

A subgroup analysis of the POEMA cohort, a prospective multicenter microbiota study spanning 20 Dutch hospitals, investigated gastrointestinal microbiota dynamics that precede infected pancreatic necrosis, a complication occurring in about 30 % of necrotizing pancreatitis cases and thought to stem from intestinal bacterial translocation. By defining the microbial shifts that happen before infection, the work seeks to identify early biomarkers that could flag patients at risk of septic pancreatic necrosis. The findings, reported in Gut Microbes via PubMed, highlight the potential of microbiota monitoring to improve early detection and possibly guide preventive strategies in severe pancreatitis.

Sunflower meal extract shows promise as a sustainable functional food ingredient that can selectively influence gut microbes while delivering antioxidant, anti‑inflammatory and α‑glucosidase inhibition. Researchers obtained the phenolic‑rich extract through a simple, food‑grade, environmentally friendly process that leaves a feed‑appropriate solid residue. Chemical analysis highlighted a high phenolic profile, with chlorogenic acid as a major component, correlating with the observed bioactivities. The findings suggest that this by‑product could be repurposed to modulate individual gut bacterial growth and support metabolic health. Source: PubMed — Food chemistry

Research published in Gastroenterology shows that dietary cholesterol and its oxidized form trigger separate metabolic and gut‑microbiota responses that are pertinent to the gut‑liver axis. The study, labeled Mo2078, compared the effects of normal cholesterol versus oxidized cholesterol on host metabolism and microbial communities, finding distinct signatures that could influence liver health. These patterns may help explain links between cholesterol intake and gastrointestinal conditions such as colorectal cancer and irritable bowel syndrome, highlighting a mechanistic route by which diet shapes the gut‑liver connection.

Mol992 MICROBGWAS introduces a multi‑level analytical framework that jointly models gut microbiota composition and multi‑organ disease risk, aiming to uncover microbiome‑driven pathways linking the intestine to conditions such as colorectal cancer and irritable bowel syndrome. By integrating microbial abundance data with host‑level phenotypes across organs, the tool enables researchers to prioritize taxa and functional signatures associated with systemic disease susceptibility. Developed and validated in a gastroenterology‑focused cohort, Mol992 MICROBGWAS offers a scalable approach for translational studies seeking to translate microbiome insights into cross‑organ risk predictions. Source: Gastroenterology.

Researchers report that elevated serum bile acids (cholemia) together with specific gut‑microbial metabolites alter the osmotic resistance of red blood cells, producing a measurable shift in erythrocyte fragility that correlates with hepatobiliary dysfunction. The study, published in Gastroenterology, suggests this altered osmotic resistance could serve as a simple, bedside diagnostic marker for liver and bile‑duct disease, linking microbiome‑driven metabolic changes to a readily assayable hematologic parameter. No additional assay complexity is required, offering a potential low‑cost tool for early detection of hepatobiliary pathology.

Researchers reported in Gastroenterology that portal vein blood from patients with metabolic dysfunction‑associated steatotic liver disease contained no detectable microbiome‑derived ethanol, undermining the idea that gut bacteria generate enough alcohol to drive MASLD. The study analyzed samples from a cohort of MASLD patients using sensitive assays and found ethanol levels at or below the limit of quantification, comparable to healthy controls. This negative result suggests that endogenous ethanol production by the intestinal microbiota is not a major contributor to liver injury in this population, redirecting focus toward other metabolic pathways in MASLD pathogenesis.

The Microbiome and Insulin Longitudinal Evaluation Study (MILES) examines whether a 730‑day dietary pattern shapes the gut microbiome and insulin homeostasis in individuals with metabolic‑associated steatotic liver disease (MASLD). By tracking dietary intake, stool‑based microbial profiles, and serial insulin measures over two years, the study seeks to uncover microbial signatures that precede or accompany changes in insulin sensitivity and liver fat. Its novelty lies in coupling long‑term diet exposure with repeated microbiome and metabolic phenotyping to infer causal pathways in MASLD progression. Source: Gastroenterology.
