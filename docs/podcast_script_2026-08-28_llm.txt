Latent tuberculosis infection (LTBI) in people living with HIV reshapes the gut microbiome and its immune‑metabolic cross‑talk, according to a study in Gut Microbes indexed by PubMed. While HIV‑associated gut dysbiosis is well known, the additional impact of LTBI has been unclear. The researchers combined cross‑sectional and exploratory longitudinal analyses of PLHIV cohorts, showing that LTBI status correlates with distinct bacterial taxa shifts and altered metabolites linked to immune activation. These microbiome changes may help explain why LTBI raises the annual risk of TB reactivation by 3%–16% in this population. Source: PubMed — Gut microbes.

Researchers profiled the gut microbiome and serum metabolome of prepubertal girls with vulvar lichen sclerosus to test the gut‑skin hypothesis. Using fecal 16S rRNA sequencing on 43 VLS cases and 46 healthy controls, alongside untargeted serum metabolite analysis, they found that VLS patients harbor a distinct bacterial community structure and show shifted levels of several microbial‑derived metabolites compared with controls. These alterations suggest a dysbiotic gut environment that may influence skin immunity in this chronic anogenital condition. The study, published in Microbiology Spectrum via PubMed, provides the first integrated multi‑omics view of the gut‑skin axis in prepubertal VLS.

Using 1,150 complete bacterial genomes, researchers built genome‑scale metabolic models to map how microbes exchange metabolites in the gut. The study, published in Cell Reports via PubMed, shows that draft assemblies systematically distort these interactions and leave out essential transport proteins, leading to biased predictions of community function. By contrast, the complete‑genome approach captures the full repertoire of uptake and secretion systems, revealing a tighter link between microbial metabolism and host health. The work underscores that high‑quality reference genomes are crucial for accurate microbiome‑metabolism modeling.
