Fecal microbiota transplantation and other microbiota‑based therapeutics are emerging strategies to counteract the dysbiosis that frequently accompanies allogeneic hematopoietic stem cell transplantation, according to a review in Clinical Pharmacology and Therapeutics (PubMed). The intestinal microbiome governs immune homeostasis, metabolism and barrier integrity, and its disruption—marked by reduced diversity, pathobiont expansion and loss of beneficial metabolites—is linked to graft‑versus‑host disease, infection and poor survival. The authors summarize current clinical evidence showing that restoring a healthy microbiota can mitigate these complications, while noting gaps in standardization, dosing and long‑term safety that future trials must address.

Epidural-related maternal fever may reshape the vaginal microbiome of laboring women, with possible repercussions for newborn brain development, according to a study published in Obstetrics and Gynecology International via PubMed. Researchers enrolled pregnant individuals who had vaginal deliveries with epidural analgesia, separating them by the presence or absence of intrapartum fever. They then characterized maternal vaginal flora to determine whether fever episodes correlate with microbial shifts and whether those shifts relate to early infant neurodevelopmental outcomes. The work highlights a potential microbiological pathway linking analgesic‑associated fever to infant health.

Subclinical cholestasis emerges as a defining feature of gut dysbiosis that drives resistance to cancer immunotherapy. Researchers reporting in PubMed‑indexed Cancer Cell show that antibiotic treatment or the expansion of antibiotic‑associated Enterocloster species depletes secondary bile acids such as deoxycholic acid in the intestine. This bile‑acid loss reduces ileal expression of mucosal addressin cell adhesion molecule‑1 (MAdCAM‑1), weakening the recruitment of immune cells to the gut and blunting antitumor immunosurveillance. The study links microbial‑induced metabolic shifts directly to impaired immunotherapy response, highlighting bile‑acid restoration as a potential strategy to overcome resistance.

Machine learning reveals that natural Arabidopsis populations actively shape their leaf microbiomes to suppress the oomycete pathogen *Albugo laibachii*, pointing toward a more rational design of biocontrol strategies. The study, reported in *Nature Communications*, analyzed six consecutive generations of leaf‑associated microbial communities from wild Arabidopsis plants that had experienced *Albugo* infection. By tracking microbiome composition across these generations, the researchers found that microbial diversity was not random but was significantly influenced by three factors: the presence of infection, the geographic site where the plants grew, and the host plant’s genotype. This pattern suggests that both the pathogen and the plant’s genetic background exert selective pressures on which microbes persist, while local environmental conditions further modulate the community.

The authors interpret these dynamics as evidence that plants recruit antagonistic microbes—those that inhibit *Albugo* growth—as part of a defense response. Applying PCR cycle autonormalization to PacBio full‑length 16S rRNA library preparations improves the accuracy and uniformity of amplicon sequencing, according to a study published in mSphere via PubMed. The work highlights a practical step toward more reliable full‑length 16S rRNA profiling for microbiome studies. (Source: mSphere, PubMed)
