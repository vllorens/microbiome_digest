Microbe. The authors note that numerous cross‑sectional studies have found associations between microbial composition and cancer, but because they measure the microbiome after disease onset they cannot establish causation. By sampling stool from healthy participants and following them over time, researchers can track microbial changes that precede tumour formation and test mechanistic links. This approach will clarify whether specific microbes drive carcinogenesis or merely reflect tumour‑related alterations, advancing microbiome‑based cancer risk assessment.

The study shows that traditional PTI and ETI models, built from single‑pathogen experiments, miss how plants maintain immune balance amid complex, shifting microbiomes. By reframing immunity as a host‑microbiome network, the authors reveal that microbial signals, community assembly processes, and even transgenerational feedback loops shape plant defense outcomes. This extended view explains why plants can tolerate beneficial microbes while still resisting pathogens, highlighting molecular cross‑talk that integrates microbiome composition into immune signaling. The work, published in Microbiological Research via PubMed, calls for experiments that test microbiome‑mediated immunity in realistic, multispecies settings.
