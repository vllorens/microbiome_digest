Solid‑state fermentation markedly raises the bioaccessibility of soybean isoflavones and reshapes their interaction with the gut microbiota, according to a new study in the Journal of the Science of Food and Agriculture (PubMed). By converting isoflavone glycosides into more absorbable aglycone forms, the process boosts the amount of isoflavones that can be released during digestion. In vitro fecal incubations showed that the fermented product stimulated growth of beneficial bacteria such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium while suppressing certain opportunistic strains. These findings highlight solid‑state fermentation as a promising bioprocessing tool to enhance the functional value of soy‑based foods.

Vagal sensing of altered gut microbiota drives externalizing behaviors, according to a new study in Gut microbes (PubMed). Researchers found that changes in the intestinal microbiome activate interoceptive brain pathways via the vagus nerve, and that this signaling depends on the host protein Sirtuin 3. When Sirtuin 3 function was disrupted, the usual microbiota‑brain communication was weakened and animals showed increased impulsivity and aggression‑like actions. The work ties host‑genetic‑linked microbiome shifts to behavioral outcomes, highlighting a gut‑vagus‑brain axis that could explain why microbial changes influence mood and conduct.

Researchers have linked structural variants in the human genome to gut microbiome composition and blood metabolic traits, suggesting possible causal relationships. Using high‑coverage (~42×) whole‑genome sequencing from 2,002 individuals, they built a catalog of 138,859 high‑confidence structural variants, of which 44.3 % are variable across the cohort. By testing these variants against microbiome abundances and circulating metabolites, they identified numerous SV‑trait associations that were not captured by single‑nucleotide polymorphism studies. The work, published in Advanced Science via PubMed, highlights how larger DNA rearrangements may shape host‑microbe interactions and metabolic health.

White tea extract effectively suppresses enzymatic browning and boosts the prebiotic value of fresh‑cut lotus root, according to a PubMed‑indexed study in Food Science & Nutrition. The research shows that polyphenol oxidase, the enzyme driving lotus root discoloration, is inhibited by white tea compounds, slowing the browning reaction that limits shelf life. Simultaneously, lotus root treated with the extract exhibits enhanced fermentability, promoting growth of beneficial gut microbes in vitro. These dual actions suggest white tea could serve as a natural, food‑grade preservative that also adds functional fiber benefits to lotus‑root products.
